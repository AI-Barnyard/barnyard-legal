Investing Basics Workbook

Building your first portfolio — a plain-language workbook for someone about to open their first brokerage account.

Educational only. This workbook explains common concepts and uses widely-published rules of thumb to illustrate them. It is not personalized investment advice, and nothing here accounts for your full financial picture. For decisions that matter, talk to a licensed financial advisor or tax professional.

01What you're actually buying

Three terms cover most of what a beginner needs.

A stock
A share of ownership in one company. If that one company does badly, so do you. This is why holding only a handful of individual stocks is riskier than it looks.
An index fund
A single fund that holds hundreds or thousands of companies at once, tracking a whole market (like the S&P 500). One purchase spreads your money across everything in that index — that spread is the entire point.
A bond
A loan to a government or company that pays interest. Historically less volatile than stocks and lower returning. Its job in a portfolio is to cushion the drops, not to make you rich.

05Before you buy anything — checklist

☐ High-interest debt handled first
A credit card at 24% reliably costs you more than a portfolio is likely to make. Paying it down is the higher-certainty return.
☐ Emergency fund exists, separately
If an unexpected bill forces you to sell during a downturn, you've locked in the loss. Cash reserve is what prevents that.
☐ Employer match captured
If your job matches 401(k) contributions, that match is part of your compensation. Not taking it is leaving pay on the table.
☐ You know the fund's expense ratio
An expense ratio is the annual fee, as a % of your balance. The difference between 0.03% and 0.75% compounds into real money over decades.
☐ You have a contribution schedule, not a timing plan
Deciding "$X on the 1st of each month" removes the hardest repeated decision — when to buy.
